Historical Roots: Surveillance Pre-Dating the Digital

Contextualizing surveillance capitalism through exploring it's long lineage in previous forms of business control.

An Infrastructure of Predictability and Control

Surveillance capitalism is driven by the desire to manage and control risk, whether it's predicting customer purchases, worker efficiency, or creditworthiness. This process is a central feature of American capitalism and has been ongoing for more than a century.

The main purpose of intensive data collection is the "regularization of behaviour" to erase uncertainty from the capitalist system.

Kenneth Lipartito (2025) views surveillance not just as an activity, but as a massive infrastructure that has been built up over decades. Like the infrastructure of the automobile (roads, laws, gas stations), the surveillance infrastructure (digital networks, algorithms, data laws) becomes ubiquitous, self-reinforcing, and difficult to "opt out" of.

Early Capitalism

Surveillance was deployed through the violent coercion of slave labor to monitor, control, measure, and assess workers. Commercial surveillance has always had roots in highly coercive and physical systems of control designed to maximize output and predictability.

Additionally, Taylorist Logic was used to scrutinize industrial workers in the early 20th century. Taylorism, or scientific management, involves intensive study and measurement of workers' movements and efficiency to standardize and maximize output, thereby reducing uncertainty in the production process.

Risk, Finance, and Monetizing Personal Data

The practice of monetizing personal information and assessing risk also substantially pre-dates the digital revolution, and represent early efforts to transform personal information into commercial assets to manage financial uncertainty.

Credit reporting is highlighted as an early example of monetizing personal data. This practice was even condemned as spying as early as the mid 1800s.

Insurance companies also engaged in risk assessment, which involved gathering and monetizing personal data to predict and control future outcomes related to policies.
